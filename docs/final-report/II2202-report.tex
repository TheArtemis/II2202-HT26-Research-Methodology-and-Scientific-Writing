\reporttitle{Transparent Multi-Hop Routing for Raft Under Partial Network Partitions}
\unnumberedheading{Authors}
Lorenzo Deflorian <ldef@kth.se>\par
Juozas Skarbalius <juozas@kth.se>\par
\vspace{2pt}

\unnumberedheading{Abstract}
Partial network partitions can disrupt Raft even when nodes remain reachable through intermediate peers. We compare direct communication with transparent multi-hop forwarding beneath an unchanged HashiCorp Raft implementation across five topologies and three per-link delays. Of 900 validated trials with a 500 ms heartbeat timeout, all 450 forwarding trials satisfied the defined stable-progress criterion, compared with 309 direct trials. Forwarding improved throughput under quorum loss in T2 and produced successful commits in T3, where direct communication produced none. Successful-request latency changed little in the healthy baseline and increased when forwarded paths were required for quorum replication. In T1 and T4, forwarding also restored client access to leaders that retained a direct quorum.

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Background and Rationale}

Distributed databases, file systems, and cluster orchestration services rely on consensus to agree on a shared state.
Raft is a leader-based consensus protocol --- the leader appends client entries and replicates them to followers with an entry considered to be committed once a majority has stored it~\cite[Sec.~5.3]{ongaro2014raft}.
To make progress leader must be able to reach a majority of followers.
Otherwise, the cluster is unable to make progress until new leader is elected.
Safety is ensured under arbitrary message delay~\cite[Secs.~5.3--5.4]{ongaro2014raft}.

Complete network partitions are well understood---as long as there exists a majority of nodes that can communicate with each other, the entries can be committed.
\emph{Partial} partitions are different---individual links fail while nodes remain virtually reachable through intermediate nodes~\cite[Sec.~2]{alfatafta2020nifty}.
Raft assumes direct point-to-point communication links between nodes and does not forward messages through intermediate nodes~\cite[Secs.~5.1--5.3]{ongaro2014raft}.
For example, in a three node cluster, a leader that loses two direct follower links may therefore stall even though those followers remain reachable at the network layer.
A notable real-world example is the Cloudflare outage in 2020, where a partial network failure disrupted etcd's Raft cluster~\cite{cloudflare2020byzantine}.

We therefore test whether forwarding messages through surviving links restores Raft's progress and measure its latency cost.

\subsection{Theory and Related Work}

Jensen et al.\ studied etcd's Raft under partial network partitions and observed election instability and delayed requests.
They have suggested two possible solutions in the face of partial network topologies: the use of the \emph{PreVote} mechanism and network overlay routing~\cite[Sec.~5]{jensen2021raft}.
PreVote mechanism is an additional safety check in Raft where a node checks whether it could still become a leader before initiating a real election.
This prevents disrupting the cluster during partial partitions. 
However, they claim that PreVote substantially increases the commit latency during partial network partitions~\cite[Sec.~5]{jensen2021raft}.
They suggested that the overlay networks might be a better solution in terms of reduced commit latency during partial partitions~\cite[Secs.~4--5]{jensen2021raft}.
However, they did not evaluate the overlay networks in a systematic way.

Alfatafta et al.\ introduced Nifty, a transparent layer that re-routes traffic around failed paths.
Nifty masks partial partitions with low overhead in several production systems such as Apache Kafka, but not Raft~\cite[Secs.~6--7]{alfatafta2020nifty}.
OmniPaxos changes the consensus protocol to handle partial connectivity and uses quorum connectivity as a requirement during leader election.
Its authors discuss overlay routing as an alternative but note that extra hops may increase commit latency~\cite[Secs.~2,8]{ng2023omnipaxos}.

More recent work explores protocol-level remedies.
HoliPaxos integrates failure detection with MultiPaxos and suppresses leader churn, evaluating partial partitions against OmniPaxos and Raft with and without PreVote and CheckQuorum~\cite[Secs.~3.1, 4.3]{liang2025holipaxos}.
RaftOptima modifies Raft's election behaviour to limit repeated elections and introduces proxy leaders for replication scalability~\cite[Secs.~IV--V]{kondru2024raftoptima}.
Qsync addresses partial partition livelock in the leaderless Rabia consensus protocol through request queue synchronization~\cite[Secs.~4--5]{kida2026qsync}.
Complementary work provides automated partition testing through Slicify~\cite{khaleel2024slicify} and probabilistic analysis of communication loss and Raft latency~\cite{li2026consensusnetwork}.

The reviewed studies leave open how forwarding beneath an unchanged Raft implementation affects progress and commit latency across different partial-partition topologies.
\subsection{Research Problem, Aim, and Research Questions}

\textbf{Research problem.}
The liveness benefit and commit latency cost of applying network-layer multi-hop routing to Raft have not been experimentally characterized.

\textbf{Aim.}
Measure whether transparent multi-hop forwarding restores progress in the tested Raft topologies and how it affects client-observed commit latency, without modifying the consensus implementation.

\textbf{Research questions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{RQ1 (Liveness):} Across topologies T1--T4, in which cases does transparent multi-hop routing restore stable progress in Raft compared with direct communication only?
  \item \textbf{RQ2 (Latency):} How does added multi-hop overlay affect client-observed commit latency?
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Hypotheses.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{H1:} Network overlay restores progress during partial partitions in topologies where leader still can reach majority quorum via intermediate nodes.
  \item \textbf{H2:} Commit latency increases during partial partitions where leader needs to reach majority quorum via intermediate nodes. During normal operation, little to no latency overhead is expected.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Research Method}

\subsection{Research Design and Methodological Rationale}

We answer both research questions through a controlled quantitative experiment with systematic network failure injection.
The network is emulated using Mininet and is populated by required number of Raft nodes and links for each tested topology.
In order to answer both research questions, we need to establish the behavior of Raft cluster under the same partial network topologies using default point-to-point links only.
Thus, we have to repeat each experiment with both direct communication mode (using point-to-point links only) and forwarding mode (shortest route to the next available alternative).
We decided to use some of the partial network topologies from Omnipaxos paper ~\cite{ng2023omnipaxos} as they expose scenarios under which leader can still reach majority quorum via intermediate nodes.
In addition, same experiments are repeated with different artificial per-link delays.
Other parameters and conditions such as computing resources, heartbeat timeout, client workload rate, payload size and observation periods are kept constant.
This design aims to isolate the effect of multi-hop communication within each topology and delay.

Each experiment run consists of the warmup period allowing the cluster to elect stable leader. 
The client is started which sends requests to the cluster at the constant rate, and awaits and records the timestamps of acknowledged requests.
After such period, required links are cut to match the topology under test.
Afterwards, the cluster is observed for $30$ seconds and results are collected.
However, we acknowledge that there might be some variability between the identical runs due to network scheduling, runtime warmup unpredictability and other factors.
To minimize the impact of such variability in our results, we repeat each experiment run $30$ times.
This is known systems benchmarking strategy that uses repetitions allowing researches to be aware of measurement uncertainty~\cite{kalibera2013rigorous}.

The design is a comparison of communication mode (\(\mathrm{direct}\) vs.\ \(\mathrm{forwarding}\)) across five predefined topologies (T0--T4) and selected per-link delays.
T0 is a healthy baseline and T1 a control in which the leader retains a direct majority after a non-critical cut. T2--T4 instantiate quorum-loss, constrained election, and chained connectivity.
For RQ1, we observe whether commit progress is maintained or recovered after failure injection in each topology under both modes.
For RQ2, we compare client commit latency when a forwarded path is needed for the deciding majority with control cases that retain a direct quorum.

We considered several alternatives and rejected them as less suitable for the stated questions.
First, adaptive overlays such as Nifty combine failure detection, route discovery, and forwarding~\cite{alfatafta2020nifty}.
Using Nifty would have confounded the effect of multi-hop path availability with discovery and installation latency. Instead, we use Mininet so that the treatment is path availability and delay alone.
Second, protocol-level remedies (PreVote, OmniPaxos-style quorum connectivity, HoliPaxos) change consensus behaviour rather than the network underneath an unchanged Raft~\cite{jensen2021raft,ng2023omnipaxos,liang2025holipaxos}.
Those approaches answer a different question. Our aim is to evaluate a network-layer remedy without modifying the consensus implementation.
Therefore we opted for an executable Mininet deployment for network emulation with HashiCorp Raft for the consensus cluster~\cite{hashicorp_raft,lantz2010mininet}.

We assume that preconfigured forwarding correctly realizes the intended network topology (T0--T4), that Mininet link delay overhead is negligible compared to artificial per-link delay, and that client observed latencies are sufficient for measuring commit latency.
We constrain the study to HashiCorp Raft with a fixed heartbeat timeout (\(500\,\mathrm{ms}\)), an open-loop client (\(100\) requests/s, \(64\,\mathrm{B}\) payloads), and a \(30\,\mathrm{s}\) client observation period after setup.

Relative to the research plan, the methodological core---controlled Mininet experiment, direct vs.\ preconfigured forwarding, topologies T0--T4, and delay factors---is unchanged.
Two practical changes were made.
The plan intended to run all trials on a single personal machine. To execute all experiments within the course deadline we moved execution to AWS EC2 instances, with each instance uploading Raft logs, client logs, and metrics to S3 for later merge.
We also fixed the heartbeat timeout at \(500\,\mathrm{ms}\) rather than testing against multiple timeout values, so that delays can be interpreted against one baseline.

\subsection{Experimental Platform and Conditions}

We ran repeated HashiCorp Raft trials in Mininet under fixed topology, communication mode, and per-link delay~\cite{hashicorp_raft,lantz2010mininet,mininet230}.

Since HashiCorp Raft is a Go library and Mininet exposes Python API, we implemented Mininet Go wrapper with ability to define Mininet configuration using Go and Yaml files.
Go then relays this configuration to Mininet server through RPC.
Surviving point-to-point links for each topology are defined in respective Yaml files for convenience.
Experiment controller, upon the start of the run, creates a complete topology (without partitions), performs basic health and leader stability checks, and then cuts down links to match tested topology.
A basic overlay routing is implemented to reroute traffic around failed links through intermediate nodes using BFS algorithm and chooses first viable hop.

In direct communication mode, replicas use point-to-point links only.
In forwarding mode, requests are forwarded through shortest-path alternative if the corresponding point-to-point link is not available.
Heartbeat timeout is fixed at \(500\,\mathrm{ms}\).
We used HashiCorp Raft with its optional PreVote mechanism disabled in all trials.
Trials ran on sixteen AWS \texttt{c5a.large} EC2 instances in \texttt{eu-north-1}.
Each instance uploaded client logs, Raft events, and metrics to an S3 bucket for later merging and filtering.
Commit latency is measured using client-observed commit timestamps.

\subsection{Work Procedure and Data Collection}
\label{sec:procedure}

Each trial follows a fixed sequence:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Initialize Mininet topology with artificially introduced delays.
  \item Start the fully connected cluster, allow a natural election, and record the actual leader.
  \item Start the open-loop client.
  \item Warm up \(30\,\mathrm{s}\) (require at least \(\geq 1\) successful commit).
  \item Cut links to match the partial network topology, remapping the planned cuts around the actual leader.
  \item Observe for \(30\,\mathrm{s}\).
  \item Archive client logs, Raft events, timeline, and host load.
  \item Tear down before the next run.
\end{enumerate}

For T3, warm-up is skipped to preserve divergent logs. After injection, replicas are stopped, reseeded with the longest log on the isolated tip holder, and restarted under the partition. The client is moved to a majority-side host and starts after health and leader checks. The recorded injection-to-first-commit time includes these operations.

For other topologies the client remains on the topology's configured initial-leader host even if the naturally elected leader is a different replica. A failed direct link can therefore prevent the client from reaching a leader that still has a quorum.

Deviations from the research plan are the use of AWS EC2 infrastructure instead of a single local VM, heartbeat timeout fixed at \(500\,\mathrm{ms}\), and repetitions raised to \(30\).

Forwarding routes and failed-link sets are taken from topology YAML files so both modes share peer addresses and differ only in path availability.

\subsection{Data Analysis and Quality Assurance}

For RQ1, within each topology we compare direct vs.\ forwarding on client-observed commit throughput, recovery time, term counts, and client failure rate.
$T0$ and $T1$ are control topologies mostly used to test our experimental setup and configuration.
Primary measured variables are commit throughput and recovery time.
For RQ2, we take the median and 95th percentile client commit latency per run and contrast cells where a forwarded follower is needed for the deciding majority (notably T2) with direct-quorum controls (T0 and T1).
When only forwarding is live (T3), latency is reported for that mode alone.
With $n=30$ per condition we report means and sample standard deviations rather than formal hypothesis tests. Latency summaries exclude runs without successful observe requests their sample sizes are reported separately.
Throughput divides successful client commits by the recorded interval from injection to observation end. This interval includes post-injection validation and, for T3, replica restart and setup before the client starts. We additionally report T3 throughput using the interval from client start to observation end.
A validated trial means that setup, warmup, connectivity checks, and artifact collection completed. The stable-progress indicator requires a successful client commit, a recorded recovery, and an election-event count no greater than $\max(5,\lfloor N_{\mathrm{ok}}/10\rfloor+3)$. It is a descriptive heuristic, not a proof of consensus liveness.
Validity is supported by paired conditions, fixed binaries and seeds, post-inject connectivity checks, host-load monitoring, and exclusion of invalid warm-ups.

\subsection{RQ1: Client progress and recovery}

T0 sustains progress in all \(180\) runs, with throughput between \(99.75\) and \(100.00\) commits/s across modes and delays. 
No further elections were observed in this topology after warmup.
Mean time to first successful commit range from \(4.43\) to \(14.53\,\mathrm{ms}\). 
No warmup election events were recorded in any T0/T1/T2/T4 trial confirming the stability of the experimental Raft setup.

In T1, forwarding maintains stable progress in all \(90\) runs and averages \(99.70\)--\(99.79\) commits/s. 
Meanwhile, direct mode has zero successful observe requests in \(2/30\), \(4/30\), and \(7/30\) runs at \(0.5\), \(5\), and \(20\,\mathrm{ms}\), respectively. 
Its corresponding mean throughputs are \(93.20\), \(86.53\), and \(76.50\) commits/s. 
All \(13\) zero-throughput runs have initial leader E, while the client remains on C. 
The C--E link is cut. 
In inspected event logs, the leader retains a quorum while the client cannot reach it. 
These observations establish an end-to-end availability benefit, without establishing a consensus liveness failure.

T2 provides the clearest throughput contrast where the initial leader loses a direct quorum.
At \(0.5\,\mathrm{ms}\), direct throughput is \(63.34\pm11.99\) commits/s and forwarding reaches \(96.93\pm1.82\).
At \(5\,\mathrm{ms}\), the values are \(52.27\pm26.43\) and \(95.78\pm2.36\). 
At \(20\,\mathrm{ms}\), they are \(55.88\pm22.28\) and \(94.46\pm1.33\).
Forwarding satisfies the stable progress in all \(90\) runs, compared with \(79/90\) for direct mode. 
Mean recorded election counts are \(4.10\), \(5.83\), and \(7.50\) with forwarding, versus \(19.97\), \(74.00\), and \(52.33\) with direct communication.
Mean time to first succesful commit are \(20.70\), \(147.88\), and \(261.03\,\mathrm{ms}\) with forwarding, compared with \(940.59\), \(426.60\), and \(396.05\,\mathrm{ms}\) with direct communication.

In T3, direct communication produces no successful client commits in any of the \(90\) runs and has a \(100\%\) client failure rate.
Forwarding makes stable progress in all \(90\) runs. 
Mean throughput using injection-to-end timing is \(59.48\pm0.06\), \(58.96\pm0.10\), and \(57.45\pm0.11\) commits/s at the three delays.
These values include setup before the client starts. 
From the start of the client requests, the corresponding mean throughputs are \(99.97\), \(99.86\), and \(99.48\) commits/s.
Mean time to first succesful commit are \(20.44\pm0.05\), \(20.87\pm0.08\), and \(22.12\pm0.10\,\mathrm{s}\) (Figure~\ref{fig:t3-rec})
The client itself starts approximately \(20.42\), \(20.81\), and \(21.95\,\mathrm{s}\) after injection. 
These recovery measurements include replica restart, health checks, and leader probing and cannot be interpreted as election delay alone.

In T4, forwarding averages \(99.75\)--\(99.94\) commits/s and maintains stable progress in all \(90\) runs.
Direct throughput averages \(69.99\), \(63.31\), and \(76.51\) commits/s at \(0.5\), \(5\), and \(20\,\mathrm{ms}\). 
There are \(9/30\), \(11/30\), and \(7/30\) zero-throughput runs, respectively. All \(27\) have initial leader C with the client on B and the B--C link cut.
The three-node majority still has size two, so C can retain a quorum with A. 
Direct mode satisfies the stable progress heuristic in \(63/90\) runs.

\subsection{RQ2: Commit latency}

In T0, forwarding changes mean median latency from \(2.42\) to \(2.53\,\mathrm{ms}\) at \(0.5\,\mathrm{ms}\) delay, from \(19.27\) to \(19.60\,\mathrm{ms}\) at \(5\,\mathrm{ms}\), and from \(83.16\) to \(84.66\,\mathrm{ms}\) at \(20\,\mathrm{ms}\).

T2 shows a larger cost where forwarding supports the deciding majority.

T1 and T4 also have higher forwarding latency, but their direct latency summaries exclude zero-throughput runs. 

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Interpretation and Answers to the Research Questions}

For RQ1, all \(450\) forwarding trials reached stable commit progress, compared with \(309/450\) direct trials, under the tested \(500\,\mathrm{ms}\) heartbeat timeout. 
In T2, forwarding mean throughput is approximately \(53\%\), \(83\%\), and \(69\%\) higher at \(0.5\), \(5\), and \(20\,\mathrm{ms}\), respectively, with fewer recorded election events. 
In T3, forwarding produces successful client commits in all \(90\) trials, while direct communication produces none. 
These observations support H1 within the tested topologies and workload. 

While in topologies T0-T2 and T4 both communication modes reach stable commit progress, with point-to-point links only lower commit throughput is observed.
This can be attributed to the fact that after introducing partial partitions, more elections are held and thus more time is spent on the leader election process.
T3, the constrained-election scenario, differs because direct communication fails to restore commit progress within the observation period. 
In this scenario, replicas have divergent logs, and Raft's voting rule requires a candidate's log to be at least as up to date as that of each replica granting its vote.
Consequently, connectivity to a majority is insufficient if the reachable replicas reject the candidate because its log is outdated. 
Under the tested partition, replicas with sufficiently up-to-date logs cannot obtain a majority of votes, while reachable candidates with older logs cannot secure the required votes.
Multi-hop forwarding restores the communication paths needed for an eligible candidate to obtain a majority and resume commit progress.

For RQ2, T0 shows small absolute differences in mean median latency, while T2 shows larger increases when forwarded paths support quorum replication. 
This is consistent with H2 and an availability/latency trade-off under the tested partial partitions. 
Successful-request latency excludes failures and must be interpreted together with client failure rate and the number of contributing runs, especially in T1 and T4 where direct-mode samples exclude zero-throughput trials.

The observations are consistent with earlier proposals to mask partial partitions through network detours~\cite{jensen2021raft,alfatafta2020nifty,ng2023omnipaxos}.

\subsection{Validity, Limitations, and Generalizability}
We used fixed software versions, predefined seeds, and a fixed heartbeat timeout, with the same EC2 instance type, network configuration, and operating system across workers.
Hardware and scheduling variability could still affect the measurements. We repeated each condition \(30\) times and report the variation between runs.

Each trial hosts all replicas and its client on a single EC2 instance. The sixteen instances run independent trials in parallel.
The emulated path delays are static while production delays can vary depending on traffic and congestion.

We did not test replicas distributed across machines or geographical regions.

Client placement can confound client availability with consensus liveness in T1 and T4. T3 injection-to-first-commit measurements include harness setup, and the stable-progress indicator is a heuristic based on client commits and recorded election events. Event counts come from sampled instrumentation and should not be treated as an exhaustive count of every internal Raft transition.

\subsection{Ethics, Societal Aspects, and Sustainability}

Our work uses open-source software and artificial workloads only.
No personal data or human participants are involved.
The results are relevant to developers and operators of Raft-based services. In the tested conditions, forwarding restored client access or improved progress, but increased successful-request latency when quorum replication used forwarded paths.
Operators would need to test this trade-off with their own workloads and network delays before deployment.
If these availability gains carry over to deployment, dependent services could face fewer interruptions, although added commit latency could slow operations that wait for consensus.

The campaign used sixteen AWS EC2 instances. We did not quantify its energy consumption or emissions, so we cannot assess its environmental impact from the trial results alone.
Small pilot campaigns and shutdown of idle instances could reduce experimental resource use. In addition, restricting detours to failed direct paths could limit extra network traffic in deployment.

\subsection{Implications and Future Work}

The experiments provide throughput and latency measurements for forwarding beneath HashiCorp Raft in five predefined topologies. In T4, forwarding restored client access even though the leader retained a direct quorum.

A follow-up experiment should control client-to-leader reachability and measure recovery separately from harness setup. This would help distinguish client availability from consensus progress and isolate the time Raft takes to recover.
Further experiments could vary the heartbeat timeout to test its interaction with path delay, and distribute replicas across hosts or regions to assess forwarding under variable network conditions.

\section{Conclusion}

In a campaign of 900 validated trials with a 500 ms heartbeat timeout, all 450 forwarding trials satisfied the defined stable-progress heuristic, compared with 309 of 450 direct trials. Forwarding produced successful commits in all 90 constrained-election trials (T3), where direct communication produced none, and achieved higher mean throughput under quorum loss (T2). Mean median successful-request latency was also higher in T2 when forwarded paths supported quorum replication. T1 and T4 benefited from restored client access, although those observations alone do not establish consensus failure in direct mode.

These measurements support multi-hop routing as a mitigation for the tested partial partitions under this workload and timeout configuration. Generalization to other deployments requires further evaluation. Controlled client placement and recovery measurements that separate harness setup from protocol behaviour are needed for stronger causal conclusions.
